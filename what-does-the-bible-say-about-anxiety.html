What Does the Bible Say About Anxiety?

Scripture treats anxiety as a real and ordinary human burden — not a sin to be ashamed of, but a weight meant to be handed over rather than carried alone.

Anxiety appears throughout the Bible, and the people who feel it are rarely the villains. David writes psalms in a state of panic. Elijah asks to die after his greatest victory. Even Jesus, in the garden before his arrest, is described as sorrowful and heavy. The Bible never treats worry as evidence that someone lacks faith. What it does offer is a consistent instruction: stop holding it by yourself.

The central passage: Philippians 4:6-7

If there is one text people return to on this subject, it is Paul's letter to the Philippians, written while he was imprisoned.

Be careful for nothing; but in every thing by prayer and supplication with thanksgiving let your requests be made known unto God. And the peace of God, which passeth all understanding, shall keep your hearts and minds through Christ Jesus. Philippians 4:6-7, King James Version

The phrase "be careful for nothing" uses an older sense of the word. It does not mean be reckless; it means be full of care for nothing — do not let anxiety take up residence. Notice the structure of the instruction. Paul does not say the worry will disappear, and he does not promise the circumstance will change. He says the outcome is peace that guards the mind, and he describes that peace as something beyond understanding, meaning it does not depend on the situation making sense yet.

Anxiety is meant to be transferred, not suppressed

Two short verses make the same point with a physical image — something heavy being moved off one person and onto another.

Casting all your care upon him; for he careth for you. 1 Peter 5:7
Cast thy burden upon the LORD, and he shall sustain thee: he shall never suffer the righteous to be moved. Psalm 55:22

Both use the verb "cast," which is an act of throwing something away from yourself. This is worth sitting with, because a lot of religious advice about worry amounts to "stop feeling that." Scripture asks for something different and more achievable: name the thing, say it out loud in prayer, and deliberately put it down. The reason given in 1 Peter is not that the burden is small. It is that someone else cares about it.

Worry borrows trouble from a day that has not come

In the Sermon on the Mount, Jesus addresses anxiety directly and at length, and his argument is largely practical.

Take therefore no thought for the morrow: for the morrow shall take thought for the things of itself. Sufficient unto the day is the evil thereof. Matthew 6:34

A few verses earlier he asks which of his listeners can add a single cubit to his height by worrying. The point is not that tomorrow holds nothing difficult — he plainly says the day has its own trouble. The point is that anxiety spends today's strength on a problem that has not arrived, and often will not arrive in the shape we imagine.

Fear and the promise of presence

Where Scripture addresses fear rather than worry, the reassurance offered is almost always presence rather than explanation.

Fear thou not; for I am with thee: be not dismayed; for I am thy God: I will strengthen thee; yea, I will help thee; yea, I will uphold thee with the right hand of my righteousness. Isaiah 41:10
Peace I leave with you, my peace I give unto you: not as the world giveth, give I unto you. Let not your heart be troubled, neither let it be afraid. John 14:27

Jesus distinguishes his peace from the kind the world gives. The world's version usually depends on everything being settled. He offers peace that arrives before the situation resolves, which is precisely what anxiety says is impossible.

What Scripture says about speaking to someone

One of the most quietly practical verses on this subject is a proverb.

Heaviness in the heart of man maketh it stoop: but a good word maketh it glad. Proverbs 12:25

Anxiety physically bends a person, and the remedy named here is a word from someone else. The Bible does not treat this as a private struggle to be handled in isolation. Throughout Scripture, burdens are carried in community — spoken aloud, shared, and answered.

What the Bible does not say

It is worth being clear about a few things Scripture does not teach, because they get attached to these verses anyway.

It does not say that anxiety is a sin. It does not say that a faithful person will stop feeling afraid. It does not say that prayer replaces practical help — the same Bible that tells people to cast their cares also records Elijah being given food and sleep before he was given a word from God. And it does not promise that the peace described arrives instantly or permanently. Paul writes about a peace that guards the heart, which is the language of an ongoing defense, not a single event.

Persistent anxiety can also be a medical condition, and taking that seriously is not a failure of faith. If worry is interfering with sleep, work, or relationships over a long period, speaking with a doctor or counselor is a reasonable and responsible step alongside prayer.
